Subject-Verb Agreement: Comprehensive Study Guide

I. Understanding Subject-Verb Agreement

Definition: Subject-verb agreement (also known as "concord") refers to the grammatical rule that the verb in a sentence must match its subject in number and person. This primarily means a singular subject takes a singular verb, and a plural subject takes a plural verb.

Importance: Correct subject-verb agreement is crucial for clear and effective communication in English. Errors in agreement can signal a lack of mastery over the language.

Core Principle: The general rule is to use a plural verb with a plural subject and a singular verb with a singular subject. However, there are numerous exceptions and specific rules to consider.

II. Key Rules of Subject-Verb Agreement

A. Basic Principles:

  1. Rule 1: Singular vs. Plural Subjects and Verbs:
    A singular subject (noun or pronoun) takes a singular verb. (e.g., Rachel spends her free time.)
    A plural subject takes a plural verb. (e.g., Blaine and Kurt play the piano.)
    In the present tense, third person singular verbs typically end in "-s" or "-es." (e.g., He runs, She eats.)
  2. Rule 2: The Verb "To Be":
    The "be" form of verbs changes based on the number and person of the subject.
    I am confident.
    We are confident.
    You are confident.
    He/She/It is confident/amazing.
    They are confident.
  3. Rule 3: "Have" and "Has":
    All singular subjects use "has" in the present perfect tense. (e.g., Swetha has a pet dog.)
    All plural subjects use "have." (e.g., I have a younger brother, You have taken the wrong cut.)

B. Compound Subjects:

  1. Rule 4: Subjects Joined by "And":
    Two or more subjects joined by "and" are considered plural and require a plural verb.
    (e.g., Krish and Radha are on their way.)
  2. Rule 5: Subjects Joined by "Or" or "Nor":
    When one or more nouns are joined by "or" or "nor," the verb agrees with the subject closest to it.
    (e.g., Neither Ricky nor Gina is here yet; Neither the teacher nor the students have to take an initiative.)

C. Indefinite Pronouns and Special Nouns:

  1. Rule 6: Indefinite Pronouns (Always Singular):
    Pronouns like anybody, anyone, each, either, everybody, everyone, neither, nobody, no one, nothing, somebody, someone, something are usually singular and take a singular verb.
    (e.g., Everyone was happy with the outcome.)
  2. Rule 7: Indefinite Pronouns/Nouns (Context-Dependent):
    Words like all, half, some, none, most, part can be singular or plural depending on the object of the prepositional phrase.
    (e.g., Some of the students are gone; Some of the cake is gone.)
  3. Rule 8: Nouns Ending in "-s" but Singular:
    Some nouns like economics, mumps, measles, news end in "-s" but are considered singular.
    (e.g., Economics is her favorite subject; The news of demonetisation shocks the entire nation.)

D. Other Grammatical Structures:

  1. Rule 9: Units of Measurement:
    When the subject refers to a period of time, distance, money, or weight as a unit, use a singular verb.
    (e.g., Ten pounds of chocolate is too much; 10 years is not considered optimum.)
  2. Rule 10: Questions and "There/Here" Sentences:
    In questions or sentences beginning with "there" or "here," the verb often precedes the subject. The verb must still agree with the subject that follows it.
    (e.g., Where is my sweater? There are my sweaters.)
  3. Rule 11: Prepositional and Verbal Phrases:
    The subject of a verb is never found within a prepositional or verbal phrase. Ignore these phrases when identifying the true subject.
    (e.g., The mother duck (with all of her little ducklings) walks to the store.)
  4. Rule 12: Gerunds as Subjects:
    Gerunds (words ending in "-ing" used as nouns) can be subjects and follow the same agreement rules.
    (e.g., Running with ducks is my favorite sport; Running to the store and flying through the air are my favorite sports.)
  5. Rule 13: Relative Pronouns (Who, That, Which):
    When using who, that, or which, look to the noun these pronouns refer to to determine if the verb should be singular or plural.
    (e.g., The girls who eat cake are happy; The girl who eats cake is happy.)
  6. Rule 14: Collective Nouns:
    A collective noun (e.g., family, group, committee, class) takes a singular verb if the group acts as a single unit.
    (e.g., My family is settled in Australia.) If the individuals within the group are acting separately, it takes a plural verb.
    (e.g., All groups of participants have arrived.)
  7. Rule 15: Adjectives like "All," "Lots of," "Some":
    In sentences with these adjectives, the verb is used according to the noun that immediately follows it.
    (e.g., All of my dresses have become tight; A lot of food is left out.)
  8. Rule 16: "Each" or "Every" as Subject:
    When a sentence begins with "each" or "every" as the subject, it is considered singular, and the verb is singular.
    (e.g., Each student has been asked; Every teacher, parent, and student is expected.)
  9. Rule 17: Expressing Wish or Request:
    When expressing a wish or request, verbs are used differently.
    (e.g., I wish I were a bird; We request that everyone make their choices now.)

IV. Answer Key

The team of scientists conducts research on climate change. (The subject "team" is singular.)
Neither the manager nor the employees are happy with the new policy. (The verb agrees with the closest subject, "employees," which is plural.)
Every one of the students has completed the assignment. ("Every one" is a singular indefinite pronoun.)
Ten kilometers is a long distance to walk in this heat. (Units of measurement are treated as singular when referring to a single unit.)
There are many reasons why he left. (The verb agrees with the actual subject "reasons," which is plural, following "there.")
The news about the election results was surprising. ("News" is a singular noun, despite ending in 's'.)
Running and swimming are her favorite exercises. (Two gerunds joined by "and" form a plural subject.)
The committee has decided to postpone the vote. (Collective noun "committee" acts as a single unit.)
All of the books are on the shelf. (The verb agrees with "books," which is plural, following "all.")
Does anyone know where the keys are? (The verb "are" should agree with the plural subject "keys" in the implied question.)

VI. Glossary of Key Terms

Agreement
In grammar, the correspondence of a word with another in respect of number, gender, or person. For subject-verb agreement, it specifically means the verb's form matches its subject's number and person.
Collective Noun
A noun that refers to a group of individuals (e.g., family, team, committee, audience). It can take a singular or plural verb depending on whether the group is acting as a unit or as separate individuals.
Concord
Another term for grammatical agreement, particularly subject-verb agreement.
Gerund
A verb form ending in "-ing" that functions as a noun (e.g., running, swimming, reading). When a gerund or gerund phrase acts as the subject, it typically takes a singular verb.
Indefinite Pronoun
A pronoun that does not refer to any specific person, thing, or amount (e.g., everyone, somebody, anything, none, all). Some are always singular, while others can be singular or plural depending on context.
Noun Phrase
A group of words containing a noun and words that modify it, functioning as a single unit in a sentence.
Person
A grammatical category that distinguishes between the speaker (first person: I, we), the addressee (second person: you), and others (third person: he, she, it, they).
Plural Noun
A noun that represents more than one person, place, thing, or idea (e.g., students, books, cats).
Prepositional Phrase
A group of words consisting of a preposition (e.g., in, on, at, with, about), its object (a noun or pronoun), and any words that modify the object. The subject of a sentence is never found within a prepositional phrase.
Pronoun
A word that replaces a noun or noun phrase (e.g., I, he, she, it, they, who, which).
Relative Pronoun
A pronoun (e.g., who, whom, whose, which, that) that introduces a dependent clause and relates it to an earlier noun or pronoun. The verb in the relative clause must agree with the antecedent of the relative pronoun.
Singular Noun
A noun that represents one person, place, thing, or idea (e.g., student, book, cat).
Subject
The part of a sentence that performs the action of the verb or about which something is stated. The verb must agree with its subject in number and person.
Verb
A word or phrase that describes an action, state, or occurrence. The form of the verb changes to agree with its subject.
